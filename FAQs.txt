APEX-Net FAQs
============

Questions and answers will be added here during the Q&A session.

Q: How do you validate input images, and what happens if someone uploads a
   non-CXR image?

A: The frontend first checks that the selected file is reported as an image
   and is no larger than 10 MB. The backend then checks that it is a supported,
   decodable image, is within the size and dimension limits, and runs the lung
   segmentation model. If the predicted mask does not look like plausible lungs
   on both sides, the API rejects it and asks for a clear frontal chest X-ray.
   This lung-mask test is only a heuristic, not a dedicated CXR-vs-non-CXR
   classifier: some non-CXR images can pass, and some valid X-rays can fail.
   If a non-CXR passes the checks, APEX-Net may still produce disease scores.
   Also, the current upload flow stores the image before this backend check, so
   a rejected image may already have been uploaded. A trained and independently
   tested CXR-vs-non-CXR classifier would provide a stronger gate.

Q: Why does every disease have a score? If hernia has a 56% score, does that
   mean the person has a hernia?

A: APEX-Net is a multi-label classifier: it estimates a separate score for
   each of 14 findings, which can coexist, rather than choosing exactly one
   disease. The displayed scores are sigmoid model outputs and are not
   calibrated diagnostic probabilities. A 56% hernia score does not establish
   that the person has a hernia and should not be interpreted as a diagnosis.
   Results are for research/demo use and require review by a qualified
   healthcare professional.

Q: Does this mean APEX-Net cannot detect disease? What is it useful for?

A: APEX-Net can analyze a chest X-ray and produce scores that rank how strongly
   its model associates the image with each of 14 learned findings. Those
   scores can support research, model evaluation, or a demonstration of
   multi-label classification, and may help identify images for closer review.
   They do not confirm or rule out disease in an individual patient. Clinical
   use would require rigorous independent testing, appropriate calibration and
   operating thresholds, assessment across patient groups and imaging settings,
   and review by qualified clinicians. Until then, treat it as a research
   prototype, not a diagnostic or screening product.

Q: What does the heatmap indicate?

A: The heatmap is a class-specific Grad-CAM visualization showing image regions
   that contributed relatively more to the model's score for the selected
   finding. Warmer/brighter regions indicate stronger relative model attention
   in that image; they are not disease probabilities, severity measurements,
   or confirmed lesion locations. The overlay is restricted to the predicted
   lung region, and errors in that lung mask can affect or cut off the heatmap.
   It is an approximate explanation of model behavior, not a validated
   localization result or a medical diagnosis.

Q: What is the Attention Prior Module (APAM), and how does it work?

A: In APEX-Net, each of the 14 findings has two APAM branches. One receives a
   lung-region mask; the other receives that finding's spatial prior map. APAM
   summarizes DenseNet image features globally and within the supplied mask or
   prior, learns weights for feature channels, and blends the original features
   with the spatially masked features. The two branch outputs are combined and
   used to produce that finding's score. The prior maps are dataset-level
   spatial hints generated from NIH bounding-box annotations, not exact
   boundaries for the uploaded image; classes without box annotations use a
   uniform prior. APAM guides the model's attention but does not itself
   localize or prove a disease. Its heatmaps remain approximate explanations,
   not validated lesion outlines.

Q: What is the difference between lung masks and ROI maps?

A: ROI means "region of interest." In this pipeline, the lung mask is the
   per-image segmentation produced by the lung U-Net: pixels inside its
   predicted lung regions are marked on, and pixels outside are marked off.
   The lung ROI is that mask after resizing/cropping to match the classifier's
   input or feature-map dimensions; it is used to guide attention and constrain
   the heatmap. They refer to the same lung region at different processing
   stages, not two independent annotations. Disease-prior maps are different:
   they are shared, class-specific spatial hints derived from dataset
   annotations, rather than a segmentation of the current image.

Q: Is APEX-Net reliable for preliminary diagnosis?

A: No. It should not be used to make or guide a patient's diagnosis, even as a
   preliminary diagnosis. The current system reports uncalibrated model scores
   and approximate attention maps; these do not establish or rule out disease.
   The deployed model would need rigorous independent clinical validation,
   calibration, and evaluation across patient populations and imaging settings
   before clinical use could be considered. Use it only as a research/demo
   prototype, and have medical concerns assessed by a qualified clinician.

Q: Which models does the backend use, how large are they, and what CPU/GPU
   resources are needed for inference?

A: The backend loads two PyTorch models:
   1. APEX-Net: a DenseNet-121 image-classification backbone with per-finding
      Anatomy Prior Attention Module (APAM) branches and 14 output classifiers.
      It has about 16.2 million parameters and processes a 224x224 image crop.
      Its default checkpoint is
      models/ngp6gwkn/best_model_20261006-050300.pth (about 65.5 MB).
   2. Lung U-Net: a base-32-channel U-Net for lung segmentation. Its checkpoint
      uses 256x256 input and has about 7.8 million parameters. The default
      checkpoint is models/lung_unet.pth (about 31.1 MB).
   Together they have about 24.0 million parameters (about 91 MiB of FP32
   weights in memory); this excludes PyTorch, intermediate activations, image
   arrays, and heatmap generation. The API defaults to CPU unless APEX_DEVICE
   selects an available CUDA or MPS device. A GPU is optional, not required.

   Practical starting estimates, not benchmarked minimums: use at least 4 GB
   system RAM and 2-4 modern CPU cores for one-at-a-time inference on ordinary
   X-rays; 8 GB RAM gives safer headroom for large images and the API's
   40-megapixel limit. If using a GPU, 4 GB VRAM is a reasonable starting
   target, but actual needs and speed depend on the GPU, software stack, image
   size, and request load. The API serializes model inference within one
   process, so additional simultaneous requests wait; multiple server workers
   each load another copy of both models. Top-three Grad-CAM heatmaps require
   extra model/gradient work and can make requests slower than classification
   scores alone. Measure startup RAM, peak RAM/VRAM, and latency on the intended
   host and representative images before choosing a hosting plan.
